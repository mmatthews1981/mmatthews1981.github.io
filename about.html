About Lighthouse 100%

This is a very rough draft and will be rewritten several times, probably offline.

Living in the future is weird, and developing for the web in the future is even weirder. In the era of AI, we have more access to the collective wisdom of the internet in terms of code than ever before, because the entire internet has been slurped up into "training data" for AI models and... I am struggling to finish my thoughts because my IDE keeps suggesting entire paragraphs of this essay that I did not ask for, for free. By itself, this isn't really a problem, but the interface is so finicky to interact with these suggestions that I occasionally have to delete a bunch of slop out of my text before I can finish my sentence. This is bullshit.

With a little bit of searching, I've found that I've got a Copilot Free account I didn't ask for, and I am welcome to snooze it for five minutes at a time. Now I can write freely in my IDE with only the usual intellisense suggestions popping up inappropriately and, thus is important, not getting in my fucking way.

The eagerness the AI makes code suggestions while I'm putting together a page is great. AI is incredibly good at writing code I've already written before (hang on, snoozing the ai again.) (Ok, I can add an arbitrary number of 5 minute extensions, that's better.) but the complete lack of hesitation this thing has to contribute to prose is destructive. I'm here writing because I have thoughts and ideas to share, not to fill space and check a box! I want to believe in the responsibility of individuals but the eagerness in which the slop machine has been injected into my workflow without my express permission is very concerning.

Anyway, all the internet goes into the slop machine as code, and what comes out is a distressingly complete reference to writing code for the internet. it's so complete, us web developers can now do our jobs with almost no typing, and believe you me, programmers love not typing things. There are a lot of side effects to this, some real and some predicted, but one thing I didn't expect to see was people claiming that the internet is getting more accessible.

This cannot be, I thought, if the machine is trained on a largely inaccessable internet, it would be recreating these patterns in new projects. And besides, only about a third of accessibility issues are visible to machine testing anyway, so broad claims of improving accessibility are extremely sus. It turns out, when people are talking about the improving a11y in new internet, what they mean is that AI coding agents have gotten pretty good at getting 100% Lighthouse scores, or close to it.

And, honestly? That's great. As a bare minimum metric, Lighthouse is fine. It's free, it's widely available, and as long as Google and Deque stay friends we'll have it forever and nothing ever could go wrong with that. But it's not good accessibility. It's the literal bare minimum, and frankly? I don't trust the agents not to be gaming the statistics.

That's why I've done this project, a page that reports a 100% lighthouse score that violates every WCAG SC at least once. I'm not trying to take the piss on Lighthouse or prove that it's unreliable, it's to demonstrate the limits of machine testing in accessibility, that a majority of potential issues are invisible to machine testing, and that it's pretty easy to induce false negatives in Lighthouse.

We keep reminding each other not to trust the clankers' output and we keep ignoring that advice. AI is always confidently wrong about all sorts of things, and the more naieve we are on the subject the more likely we are to believe it. We're being naive about digital accessibility in the age of ai. Don't believe the clankers, look at your own code!
